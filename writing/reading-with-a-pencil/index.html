Reading with a pencil

May 3, 2026 · 5 min read

A lightweight method for turning a book from something consumed into something you can think alongside.

A pencil slows reading by a nearly perfect amount. It adds enough friction to keep the mind in the room, but not so much that the page becomes an assignment.

I used to underline passages because they sounded important. The result was a book full of borrowed emphasis and very little record of what I had thought. Now I try to leave reactions instead: a question, an objection, a connection to something three chapters earlier.

Marks with jobs

My system has only three marks. A line beside a passage means “return.” A question mark means the argument lost me. A short note means the text changed or collided with an idea I already had. Anything more elaborate tends to turn reading into bookkeeping.

The margin is where a monologue becomes a conversation.

After finishing, I revisit only the marked pages and write a few sentences on the inside cover: the author’s central move, the idea I expect to keep, and the question the book left open.

Memory through use

This is not a system for remembering every fact. It is a way of finding a book again—of leaving handles for the future. The point of a note is not preservation but reuse.

A pristine book can be beautiful. A penciled book is biographical. It keeps two timelines at once: the argument printed at publication, and the reader meeting it, one imperfect mark at a time.

Try it with the next ten pages you read. One pencil, three marks, and a little room for disagreement.
