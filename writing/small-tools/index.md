Some tools ask you to adopt a worldview. Before you can solve the problem in front of you, there is an account to create, a workspace to organize, and a system to maintain. The tool quietly becomes another project.

I am increasingly fond of software that makes a smaller promise. A script renames a folder of files. A command turns notes into a website. A plain text format remains readable after the app that introduced it is gone.

## Small is a relationship
{: #relationship }

“Small” is not really about lines of code. It describes the relationship between a tool and its user. Can I understand what it is doing? Can I leave? Can I combine it with something else?

> A good tool extends your reach without demanding your allegiance.

Focused tools are easier to trust because their edges are visible. They leave room for improvisation. When they produce ordinary files and accept ordinary inputs, they become building blocks rather than destinations.

## The test
{: #test }

When I make something now, I ask whether it can be explained in one sentence. I ask what happens to the user’s work if the tool disappears. And I ask whether a sharp default could replace another setting.

The answers do not always lead to less software. They usually lead to software with a calmer center—and that is close enough.
