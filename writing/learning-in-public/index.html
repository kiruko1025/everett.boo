Learning in public, without performing

Jul 25, 2026 · 6 min read

A field note on sharing unfinished ideas, keeping a trail of questions, and letting curiosity—not metrics—shape the work.

There is a useful kind of embarrassment that comes from explaining something before you feel ready. It reveals the soft spots in your understanding. A sentence you cannot finish is often a question you did not know you had.

That is the best argument I know for learning in public. Not “building a personal brand,” and not broadcasting every half-thought, but leaving a legible trail through a subject: what surprised you, where you became stuck, and which explanation finally made the idea click.

A notebook with the door open

I think of public writing as a notebook with the door left open. The notebook is still primarily for the person keeping it. Its job is to sharpen attention. But because someone else might wander in, the writer adds context, defines a term, and checks an assumption. That imagined reader makes the thinking more rigorous.

The audience is useful as a constraint, but dangerous as a compass.

The trouble begins when the response to a note starts directing the next note. Metrics reward certainty, speed, and conclusions. Learning usually needs their opposites: ambiguity, patience, and questions that remain questions for a while.

Keep the unfinished edges

A useful note does not need to be comprehensive. It should say honestly where its boundary is. “Here is the model I have so far” invites revision; “Here is everything you need to know” tends to conceal it.

My preferred unit is small: one concept, one friction point, one worked example. A note that can be revised is more valuable than a grand guide that never leaves the drafts folder. Over time, these small pieces form a map—not only of a subject, but of how your mind moved through it.

A simple practice

At the end of a study session, write three lines: what changed in your understanding, what remains confusing, and what you will try next. Publish only when doing so improves the note. Remove anything that performs certainty you do not have.

The goal is not to look like a person who knows. It is to become a person who notices.

Thanks for reading. If this sparked a thought, keep it moving—write the next small note.
